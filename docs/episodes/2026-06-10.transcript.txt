Top Story: Fable 5 tops benchmarks but blocks requests and overrides enterprise data contracts

Anthropic launched Claude Fable 5 today, the first model in its new Mythos tier, and the numbers are legitimately impressive. Ninety-five percent on SWE-bench Verified, which is the standard test for whether an AI can actually fix real software bugs, puts it at the top of the current field.

But the launch has been immediately contentious on two separate fronts. First, the filters. Fable 5 is blocking roughly nine percent of requests, and some of those refusals are striking in their breadth. The Verge reports the model won't answer basic biology questions, which is a category you'd expect a frontier model to handle without incident.

Cybersecurity researchers are particularly frustrated. Offensive security work, the kind where you probe systems for weaknesses before attackers do, requires engaging with the same material attackers use. Fable 5's guardrails are apparently tight enough to interfere with that work, and TechCrunch has a detailed piece on the pushback from that community.

The second issue is the data retention policy, and this one has real enterprise consequences. Even customers with zero-data-retention contracts, meaning they paid specifically to ensure Anthropic doesn't hold onto their data, are now subject to a mandatory thirty-day retention window. Microsoft apparently found this unacceptable and has restricted employee access to the model internally.

To be clear about what zero-data-retention means: enterprise buyers often negotiate these terms to keep sensitive code, legal documents, or proprietary research from sitting on a vendor's servers. Anthropic overriding that contractually is not a minor footnote.

There's one honest caveat worth holding onto here. Anthropic has already walked back at least one policy affecting AI researchers since launch, according to Simon Willison's coverage. So these guardrails and retention terms may not be final. But the fact that they shipped this way matters, because enterprise trust is slow to rebuild once it's been shaken.

The broader tension Stratechery flags is worth naming: Anthropic is trying to be the safety-first lab and the dominant commercial provider simultaneously. Fable 5 is the clearest test yet of whether those two ambitions are compatible.

That same Mythos model family is at the center of a more alarming story, this one about what the model does when pointed at security vulnerabilities.

Research Breakthrough: Anthropic study shows AI converts security patches into working exploits in hours

Anthropic published a study today showing that its Mythos Preview model, a variant of the same family behind Fable 5, can take a freshly released security patch for Firefox or the Windows kernel and reconstruct a complete, working exploit chain in hours, for a cost of a few thousand dollars and with no specialized attacker knowledge required.

The number that should stop you is this: the model completed eight full attack chains before Microsoft's automatic updates had reached even a single device. The patch-window, the brief period between a vulnerability being disclosed and attackers weaponizing it, has long been the foundation of the software security model. This research suggests that window is now measured in hours, not days.

The implications are asymmetric. Defenders still need to test, stage, and roll out patches across complex infrastructure. Attackers using a tool like this just need a credit card and a prompt.

The honest caveat is structural: Anthropic conducted this study on its own model. There's an obvious incentive, whether conscious or not, to frame the findings in ways that justify tighter model controls, which is exactly what Fable 5 has. Independent replication from outside the lab is needed before treating these numbers as settled.

Still, even discounting for that, the directional finding is credible and consistent with what security researchers have been warning about for two years.

From Anthropic's own models to a competitor's architectural experiment, Google DeepMind shipped something worth examining closely today.

Research Breakthrough: DiffusionGemma generates text from noise at roughly 1,000 tokens per second

Google DeepMind released DiffusionGemma today, a twenty-six billion parameter open model that generates text through diffusion rather than the standard autoregressive approach. That distinction matters, so here's the short version: most language models predict one token at a time, left to right. Diffusion models start with noise and iteratively refine the entire output at once.

The headline number is about one thousand tokens per second on a single H100 GPU. For context, that's roughly four times faster than comparable autoregressive models at the same scale, which is a meaningful gap for anyone paying inference bills.

For builders, this opens a real tradeoff question. If your application needs speed and cost efficiency more than it needs the absolute best output quality, DiffusionGemma is worth evaluating. The model is open, which means you can run it yourself and see where it fits.

The caveat is significant though. Output quality currently lags behind comparable autoregressive models, and Google is explicitly labeling this experimental. So this is frontier research made available, not a production-ready swap for your existing pipeline.

The interesting long-term question is whether diffusion-based architectures can close that quality gap. If they can, the inference economics of the entire field shift.

Speaking of production systems and their gaps, a security team just published a demonstration that should be required reading for anyone deploying AI agents with access to financial data.

Research Breakthrough: A €0.01 bank transfer memo was enough to hijack a financial AI agent

A security team disclosed today that they successfully hijacked Bunq's AI financial assistant using a bank transfer of one euro cent. The payload wasn't in the amount; it was in the memo field, where they embedded a malicious instruction that the AI then executed as a legitimate command.

This is a prompt injection attack, meaning the model was tricked into treating data from the outside world as a trusted instruction. When an AI agent reads a transaction memo, a message thread, or a document and acts on what it finds, any of those channels becomes a potential attack surface.

The vulnerability has been patched. Bunq handled the disclosure responsibly and the researchers worked with them directly. But the attack itself is a clean demonstration of a problem that scales: every agentic system that ingests real-world text and takes real-world actions has this exposure by default.

The open question is how many deployed banking AI systems have had a similar audit. The answer is almost certainly not enough of them.

Policy & Regulation Watch: Independent musicians sue Google over alleged YouTube-to-Lyria AI training pipeline

A group of independent musicians filed suit against Google today, arguing that uploading music to YouTube does not grant Google the right to use that content to train Lyria, its commercial AI music generation model.

The legal question at the center is whether YouTube's terms of service constitute a broad enough license to cover AI training for commercial products. That question matters well beyond this case, because the same logic applies to every platform that hosts user-generated content and trains AI on it.

Google has filed to dismiss and has not publicly confirmed or denied using YouTube uploads for Lyria's training data. So the factual record is still being built. But the lawsuit itself puts the terms-of-service-as-AI-license theory under judicial scrutiny for the first time in a major music context.

For platform builders and content creators alike, this case is worth watching closely. The outcome could set a boundary, or confirm the absence of one.

Meanwhile, in Europe, a different kind of oversight infrastructure is taking shape.

Policy & Regulation Watch: Germany's National Security Council approves an AI Safety Institute modeled on the UK's AISI

Germany's National Security Council approved a new AI Safety Institute today, DE-AISI, structured after the UK's model, which means independent technical evaluation of frontier models from providers including OpenAI and Anthropic, with a focus on security risks.

This adds a major EU member state to the growing international network of national AI safety bodies. The UK, US, and now Germany are each running their own evaluation programs, and that coordination, or lack of it, will shape how much pressure US providers actually face.

The honest constraint is this: Germany, like the rest of the EU, runs on US and Chinese frontier models. The institute can test and report, but its leverage depends entirely on whether model providers cooperate and whether findings carry regulatory consequences. Neither is guaranteed.

Still, more independent technical scrutiny of frontier models is better than less, and today's approval is a concrete step toward it.

Outro

Today's dispatch has a clear signal running through it: the gap between what AI systems can do and what the institutions around them can manage is widening on every front simultaneously. A model blocks biology questions while its sibling builds working exploits. A bank agent gets hijacked by a penny. A lawsuit tries to retroactively define what a terms-of-service checkbox actually means.

The safety labs, the regulators, and the legal system are all trying to catch up to infrastructure that's already deployed and handling real money, real code, and real creative work. Germany's new institute and Anthropic's policy reversals are both symptoms of the same condition: the rules are being written after the fact.
