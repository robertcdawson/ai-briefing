Top Story: Trump Administration Bans Anthropic's Fable 5 and Mythos 5 Models for Foreign Nationals

The directive came fast and hit hard: the Trump administration ordered Anthropic to take its Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models offline for any foreign nationals, anywhere in the world, with little warning and a legal basis that still hasn't been fully disclosed.

Fable 5 and Mythos 5 are Anthropic's most capable cybersecurity-focused models, built specifically to help defenders find and patch vulnerabilities. Taking them offline doesn't just inconvenience researchers; it removes a serious tool from the people trying to protect infrastructure.

Cybersecurity veterans were quick to protest, and their complaint is precise: adversaries, whether nation-state hackers or criminal groups, don't wait for a government memo before using AI. Defenders do. The ban asymmetrically weakens the side it was supposed to protect.

The move also set a precedent that deserves scrutiny. The US government has now demonstrated it can unilaterally restrict a private company's frontier AI models globally, including blocking a company's own foreign-national employees from using tools they need to do their jobs.

Early reporting suggested the ban was triggered by a specific jailbreak concern, but TechCrunch has since reported the jailbreak story doesn't hold up. The actual motive, whether retaliatory, tied to export-control policy, or something else entirely, remains genuinely unclear.

The Decoder raised a pointed question: the government may be demanding something technically impossible, an LLM that cannot be manipulated under any circumstances. No such model exists. If that's the standard, every frontier model fails it.

Simon Willison noted the export-control framing here is significant: this is the first time we've seen controls applied to a specific named model set rather than a broad category of capability, which could be a template for future restrictions.

What's certain is that Anthropic had no meaningful advance notice, the legal mechanism hasn't been made public, and the company's foreign-national employees are caught in the middle. That's a lot of loose ends for an order this consequential.

From a government ban with blurry motives, we move to a corporate deal with a price tag that demands explanation.

AI Business Watch: SpaceX Acquires AI Coding Tool Cursor for $60 Billion Post-IPO

SpaceX just went public and apparently celebrated by spending sixty billion dollars on Cursor, the AI coding assistant, making this one of the largest AI acquisitions ever recorded.

The deal is denominated in SpaceX stock, which is freshly liquid after the company's blockbuster IPO. SpaceX's valuation has now ballooned to two-point-seven trillion dollars, passing Amazon, which means it had the currency to make a move like this and apparently decided to.

Cursor, for context, is the developer tool that embeds an LLM directly into a code editor to suggest, complete, and explain code. It's been a favorite among AI-forward engineers. At sixty billion dollars, it's being valued like a company that could anchor an entire enterprise AI platform.

That's the stated ambition: SpaceX appears to want to compete directly with Anthropic and OpenAI in the enterprise AI market, not just build rockets with AI assistance. The acquisition is a declaration of intent.

The skeptical read is harder to dismiss, though. A rocket and satellite company owning a coding assistant is a strategic leap that hasn't been justified with specifics yet. And the ten-billion-dollar breakup fee baked into the deal structure is unusual enough to suggest the negotiations were not straightforward.

What the deal does confirm is the valuation floor for serious AI developer tooling. If Cursor is worth sixty billion, the market is pricing in a world where AI-assisted coding becomes as essential as cloud infrastructure. Builders should take note of what that implies for every tool in their stack.

AI Business Watch: OpenAI Burned $34 Billion in 2025 as Losses Grew Nearly Eightfold

According to reporting from Ed Zitron's newsletter, citing unnamed sources, OpenAI burned through thirty-four billion dollars in 2025, with losses growing nearly eightfold year over year.

To be clear about the sourcing: OpenAI has not publicly confirmed these figures. One outlet, unnamed sources. That caveat matters. But the order of magnitude is consistent with what we know about frontier model training costs, inference infrastructure, and the company's hiring pace.

The number that should stop you is the growth rate. Eightfold in a single year is not a company scaling efficiently toward profitability; it's a company in a spending race where the finish line keeps moving.

The structural question this raises for the whole industry is whether any frontier lab can reach profitability before capital markets decide they've seen enough. OpenAI's revenue is growing, but thirty-four billion in losses means the gap is still enormous.

For builders and investors watching from the outside, the honest takeaway is uncertainty. The frontier AI economics are not yet proven to be sustainable, and the companies best positioned to survive a tightening are the ones with diversified revenue, not just API calls.

AI Business Watch: DeepSeek Raises $7.4 Billion in First-Ever External Funding Round at $50B Valuation

DeepSeek, the Chinese AI lab that sent markets into a brief panic earlier this year by releasing competitive models at a fraction of the expected cost, has taken outside money for the first time: seven-point-four billion dollars at a fifty-billion-dollar valuation.

This is a meaningful shift. DeepSeek had operated without external investors, which gave it unusual independence and made its cost efficiency claims harder to interrogate. Accepting outside capital changes that dynamic, though details on who the investors are and what governance they get remain sparse.

The fifty-billion valuation signals that institutional money still believes in non-US frontier AI development, despite the export control environment and the geopolitical friction around Chinese AI labs.

What's missing from the reporting is almost as important as what's there: no clear picture of how the capital gets deployed, no investor names, and no clarity on Chinese regulatory approval. Those are significant blanks.

The contrast with OpenAI's thirty-four billion in losses is worth sitting with. DeepSeek built a competitive lab on a fraction of that spend, and now it's raising serious money. Whether that efficiency holds at greater scale is the open question.

Product & Tool Watch: Critical Microsoft Copilot Vulnerability Let Attackers Steal Users' 2FA Codes

Researchers disclosed a critical vulnerability in Microsoft Copilot, dubbed SearchLeak, that allowed attackers to steal two-factor authentication codes directly from users.

The attack exploited a class of failure that security researchers have been warning about for years: when you embed an LLM into a workflow that handles sensitive data, the model can become a channel for exfiltrating that data, whether through prompt injection or manipulated search results.

Two-factor authentication codes are specifically designed to be short-lived and hard to intercept. Getting an AI assistant to hand them over represents a meaningful failure in the security model of the whole product.

The direct takeaway for anyone building AI agents or integrating LLMs into user-facing workflows: the attack surface isn't just the model, it's everything the model can touch. Sensitive data in context is sensitive data at risk.

Microsoft hasn't publicly confirmed whether the vulnerability is fully patched, so the residual exposure is still unclear. That's not a reassuring sentence to have to say about a product used by millions of enterprise customers.

AI Business Watch: Nvidia Raises Up to $25 Billion in Its First Bond Deal Since 2021

Nvidia is raising up to twenty-five billion dollars in bonds, its first debt issuance in five years, and the size alone makes it worth noting.

Bond markets and equity markets price risk differently. Equity investors bet on upside; bondholders want to know they'll get paid back. Nvidia going to the debt market at this scale is a signal that institutional fixed-income investors are willing to fund AI infrastructure expansion, not just venture and growth equity.

The use of proceeds hasn't been specified, and the final deal size wasn't confirmed at time of reporting. So the evidence trail goes cold right where it gets interesting.

What it does suggest is a broader shift in how AI capital expenditure might get financed going forward: less reliant on equity rounds, more integrated into traditional debt markets. That's a maturation signal for the industry, even if this particular deal's details remain open.

The final terms will tell us more about how much conviction the bond market actually has, versus how much it's just following the equity story.
