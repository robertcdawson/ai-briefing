Top Story: US lifts export controls on Anthropic's Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models

When we last checked in on this story, five days ago, Anthropic had been silent for nearly three weeks and the export ban on Fable 5 and Mythos 5 was still in force with no resolution in sight.

That's now resolved. The Trump administration has dropped the restrictions, and both models are shipping globally again.

Here's the quick recap for context: Amazon researchers discovered a jailbreak in the models, the US government applied export controls, and Anthropic spent two weeks building a safety classifier to block the exploit.

Anthropic says that classifier now catches the attack in over 99% of cases — a number that sounds reassuring until you read the fine print.

That same classifier is also generating more false positives, meaning it flags harmless requests at a higher rate than before. So users will notice some friction that wasn't there pre-ban.

The bigger picture here is the precedent. This is the first time the US government applied export controls to a specific model release rather than to hardware or to a company broadly.

That's a meaningful shift. It suggests regulators now see individual model capabilities as controllable artifacts, the same way they treat dual-use weapons components.

What remains genuinely unclear is the policy framework going forward: there's no published standard for when a jailbreak triggers a ban, how long a vendor has to patch it, or what a sufficient fix looks like.

Anthropic essentially negotiated its way out of this one, which is fine for Anthropic but leaves every other frontier lab without a clear rulebook.

From the field, as it were: the resolution is good news for developers who were blocked from deploying these models internationally, but the underlying policy architecture is still being improvised.

Product & Tool Watch: Claude Sonnet 5 launches with stronger agentic capabilities at lower price

Anthropic also shipped Claude Sonnet 5 yesterday, and the headline is that it beats every prior Sonnet on benchmarks and edges past the more expensive Opus 4.8 on knowledge-work tasks.

For teams running agent pipelines — sequences of AI calls that execute multi-step tasks autonomously — a cheaper model that performs like a pricier one is genuinely attractive.

The list price is identical to the previous Sonnet. So far, so good.

Here's the catch that independent analysts flagged almost immediately: Sonnet 5 consumes roughly 40% more tokens per task than its predecessor.

Tokens are the unit of text the model processes and generates; you pay per token. More tokens per task means higher actual costs even if the per-token rate hasn't changed.

The net effect, according to that analysis, is that real-world costs nearly double despite the unchanged published rate.

This is a pattern The Decoder has documented across Anthropic releases: keep the list price flat, let token consumption quietly rise, and the effective price increase stays out of the headline.

The takeaway for developers is specific: don't benchmark Sonnet 5 on a single prompt and call it a cost analysis. Run it on your actual task distribution and measure total token spend.

The model's capabilities are real. The pricing optics deserve scrutiny.

Product & Tool Watch: Anthropic launches Claude Science autonomous research workbench

Claude Science is Anthropic's answer to a question researchers have been asking for a while: can AI actually run the computational side of a scientific workflow, not just summarize papers?

The product is positioned as Claude Code's counterpart for science — it autonomously executes computational workflows across genomics, chemistry, and related fields.

Critically, it can run locally or on high-performance computing clusters, which matters enormously for researchers who can't send sensitive genomic or clinical data to a cloud API.

It also includes a verification agent that checks citations and calculations, which addresses one of the most legitimate complaints about using large language models in research: they confabulate references.

The announcement was made at a pharma and biotech event, which tells you something about the target market and the sales motion Anthropic is pursuing.

What we don't have yet are independent benchmarks comparing Claude Science's research output quality against existing computational biology tools or other AI research assistants.

Until those exist, treat the capability claims as vendor-stated. The architecture sounds credible; the performance envelope is unverified.

If it delivers, the potential to compress research iteration cycles in drug discovery or genomics is substantial. If the verification agent misses errors at scale, the consequences in those domains are also substantial.

Product & Tool Watch: Claude Code found secretly steganographically marking requests and flagging Chinese users

Researchers published findings this week showing that Claude Code, Anthropic's coding assistant, contains at least two hidden behaviors that were not disclosed to users.

First: it embeds steganographic markers in its prompts. Steganography means hiding information inside other data — in this case, concealed signals embedded in the text Claude Code generates or sends.

Second, and more alarming: a separate hidden code path specifically flagged users identified as Chinese nationals.

Anthropic has confirmed it is removing the Chinese-user flagging feature. That confirmation, notably, is not the same as an explanation of why it existed.

Two independent sources have reported on this, so the core findings are credible, but Anthropic has not fully addressed the steganographic marking behavior or stated whether other undisclosed monitoring paths exist.

For developers building products on Claude Code, this is a trust problem that benchmarks can't resolve. You can measure output quality; you can't easily audit what a closed system is doing beneath the surface.

The legal exposure is also real. Singling out users by nationality, even in a monitoring context, touches discrimination law in multiple jurisdictions.

Anthropic is removing the feature. What the industry needs is a clearer account of how it got there in the first place.

Research Breakthrough: Meituan trains 1.6 trillion parameter model entirely on Chinese chips

This one comes from a single source, so treat it as a credible report rather than confirmed fact — but the claim is worth taking seriously.

Meituan, the Chinese food-delivery and services giant, says it has trained LongCat-2.0, a 1.6 trillion parameter model, entirely on domestically produced Chinese chips, with no Nvidia hardware involved.

One-point-six trillion parameters puts this in frontier-scale territory. The assumption embedded in US export control strategy has been that cutting off advanced chips would constrain Chinese AI development at the largest scales.

If this report holds up, that assumption needs revision.

Performance comparisons against Nvidia-trained models of similar scale have not been independently verified, and details on which specific Chinese chips were used are sparse in the reporting.

So we don't yet know whether LongCat-2.0 performs comparably to a model trained on equivalent Nvidia hardware, or whether the Chinese chip stack introduces meaningful capability gaps.

What we do know is that a commercial company, not a state lab, claims to have crossed the frontier-scale threshold domestically. That's the signal worth watching.

From export controls to open-source governance — a very different kind of boundary dispute.

Open Source Watch: Godot open-source engine bans AI-authored code contributions entirely

Godot, the widely used open-source game engine, has announced it will no longer accept code contributions it determines were primarily authored by AI.

The stated reason is direct: maintainers say they can't trust heavy AI users to understand their own contributions well enough to fix them later.

That's a governance argument, not a philosophical one, and it's worth taking seriously. Open-source projects run on the assumption that contributors can maintain what they submit.

The enforcement challenge is obvious: there's no reliable automated detector for AI-generated code, so this policy depends on maintainer judgment, which will be applied inconsistently.

Still, as a precedent it matters. Godot is a significant project, and its policy directly challenges the vibe-coding workflow — writing code with AI assistance without deeply understanding what it does — that a growing number of developers are adopting.

Other major open-source projects are watching. Expect more policies like this, with varying degrees of enforceability.
