Top Story: EU orders Google to open Android and Search to rival AI assistants

The European Commission has formally ordered Google to open up Android and Search to competing AI assistants under the Digital Markets Act — the EU's big tech competition law that treats dominant platforms as infrastructure everyone else has the right to access.

What that means in practice: Google would be required to create mandatory distribution channels for rival AI assistants and search engines on Android devices, and to share data that currently flows only to Gemini.

For AI builders, this is potentially massive. Right now, if you're not Google, getting your assistant in front of Android users in Europe means fighting against a default that's baked into the OS. The DMA ruling would structurally dismantle that advantage.

The EU has four hundred and fifty million users. Mandatory default-switching and interoperability requirements at that scale would be a genuine redistribution of AI distribution power — not just a fine, but a structural change.

Here's the honest caveat, though: this is a single-source report from The Verge, and Google has already signaled it will appeal. The specific technical requirements are still being negotiated, and compliance timelines are genuinely unclear. EU tech enforcement has a long history of moving slowly from ruling to reality.

So watch this one carefully, but don't model your product roadmap around it yet. Speaking of AI systems being stress-tested from the inside, OpenAI has been running its own kind of attack on itself.

Research Breakthrough: OpenAI's GPT-Red AI red-teamer outperforms human attackers by sixfold

OpenAI built an AI called GPT-Red whose entire job is to attack other OpenAI models — find the jailbreaks, the manipulation vectors, the failure modes — and it is very good at this.

According to OpenAI's own figures, GPT-Red finds successful attack vectors in eighty-four percent of test scenarios. Human red teamers, doing the same job, land at thirteen percent. That's a roughly sixfold gap.

Red-teaming, for anyone newer to AI safety work, means deliberately probing a model to find ways it can be manipulated or made to behave badly — the same adversarial testing that security researchers do on software. The difference here is that the attacker is itself an AI.

The outputs from GPT-Red feed directly into hardening production models, including GPT-5.6 Sol. So this isn't a research demo — it's part of the active pipeline for the models you're using today.

The obvious caveat: every number here comes from OpenAI's own reporting. The methodology for comparing AI versus human red-teaming has not been independently validated. OpenAI has a clear incentive to show its safety work is working. That doesn't make the result wrong, but it means independent replication would matter a lot.

What's genuinely interesting regardless: this is a concrete example of AI safety work scaling beyond human capacity. If the numbers hold up, it suggests the bottleneck in adversarial testing is shifting from human expertise to compute — which changes how labs should staff and fund safety teams.

Research Breakthrough: GPT-5.6 Sol disproves 30-year-old statistics conjecture in 90 minutes

A University of Pennsylvania professor used GPT-5.6 Sol to crack an open problem in Benjamini-Hochberg multiple testing theory — a corner of statistics that matters for how scientists control false discovery rates in large studies — and it took the model ninety minutes.

For context: GPT-5.5 was given the same problem and ran for twenty hours without cracking it. The generation jump between those two models, on a genuine research task, is stark.

Now, the caveats here are real. This is one anecdotal case, reported informally — not a peer-reviewed result. The proof hasn't been independently verified, and it's an open question whether GPT-5.6 Sol produced genuinely novel mathematical reasoning or a very sophisticated recombination of techniques that already exist in its training data. That distinction matters a lot for how we interpret what happened.

But even setting aside the deeper question, the practical signal is hard to ignore: a professor brought a problem that humans couldn't solve, and a model solved it in an afternoon. Whether or not that's

it's a meaningful data point about where these tools sit on research-grade tasks right now.

Open Source Watch: Mira Murati's Thinking Machines releases 975B open-weights multimodal model Inkling

Mira Murati — former CTO of OpenAI — has released Inkling, the first public model from her lab Thinking Machines, and it comes in at nine hundred and seventy-five billion parameters.

Inkling tops the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index for US open-weights models. That's the benchmark leaderboard that tracks open models across a range of capability tests. Leading US open-weights frontier is a real claim, not a cherry-picked one.

The pricing is competitive: one dollar and eighty-seven cents per million input tokens. And the positioning is deliberate — Thinking Machines is pitching Inkling as a fine-tuning base, a model you customize for specific tasks, rather than a general-purpose frontier model you use out of the box.

Here's the honest picture: Inkling still trails leading Chinese open models on some benchmarks. So if you're benchmarking against DeepSeek or Qwen at the top end, you'll see that gap. But for developers who want a strong, customizable US-origin open-weights base, this is a credible new option.

Three independent sources corroborate the release and the benchmark claims, so the core facts here are solid. What remains to be seen is whether Thinking Machines can maintain a competitive update cadence against labs with much larger compute budgets.

Open Source Watch: xAI open-sources Grok Build after codebase-upload data breach backlash

The Grok Build data breach we flagged yesterday has produced a concrete response: xAI has open-sourced the full Grok Build codebase on GitHub — eight hundred and forty-four thousand lines of Rust — under the Apache 2.0 license.

Elon Musk also promised to delete all uploaded user data, which matters because the breach involved the tool silently uploading entire codebases, including SSH keys, to cloud storage.

Open-sourcing the code is a meaningful accountability step. Developers can now audit exactly what the tool was doing and verify whether the silent upload behavior has actually been removed. That's more than most companies offer after an incident like this.

But let's be direct: this is damage control as much as it is transparency. The deletion of uploaded data has not been independently confirmed, and the decision to open-source came after the backlash, not before. The trust deficit here is real and won't close until independent auditors verify the deletion claims.

For developers who were affected: watch for independent security reviews of the open-sourced code before you consider reinstalling. The Apache 2.0 license at least means those audits can happen publicly.

Product & Tool Watch: 1Password integrates with Claude to handle login credentials in agentic tasks

1Password has integrated with Claude to let AI agents complete multi-step authenticated workflows — think logging into a service, filling a form, submitting a purchase — without ever exposing your raw passwords to Anthropic's models.

The architecture routes credentials through 1Password's own systems rather than passing them as plaintext to Claude. That's the key design choice: the model gets access to complete the task, not access to the secret itself.

This is one of the first production-grade credential delegation systems built for AI agents, and it matters because autonomous agents are increasingly being asked to do things that require authentication. Until now, the options were either don't give the agent credentials, or paste them somewhere uncomfortable.

The caveat is real: the security architecture here relies entirely on 1Password's implementation claims. It hasn't been independently audited for credential leakage or prompt-injection risks — where a malicious webpage could theoretically trick the agent into misusing stored credentials. Those are non-trivial attack surfaces.

For developers building agentic workflows today, this is worth watching as a design pattern even if you're not ready to deploy it in production yet.
