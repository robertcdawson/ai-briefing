Top Story: Claude Fable claims counterexample to the Jacobian Conjecture

Four days ago we reported that GPT-5.6 Sol reportedly disproved a 30-year-old statistics conjecture in about 90 minutes — a single anecdotal case, still awaiting formal verification.

Now a researcher going by the handle alpoge posted on social media that Anthropic's Claude Fable has produced a counterexample to the Jacobian Conjecture, a problem that has resisted mathematicians for decades.

The Jacobian Conjecture, in plain terms, asks whether a certain class of polynomial maps that look invertible at a local level are actually invertible globally — a deceptively simple question that has defeated serious attempts since 1939.

A counterexample would mean the answer is no, and it would be a landmark result in algebraic geometry, not a minor footnote.

Here's the critical qualifier: this comes from one social media post. The mathematics community has not independently verified it. No preprint has appeared on arXiv. No peer review has occurred.

History is littered with claimed proofs and disproofs of the Jacobian Conjecture that later collapsed under scrutiny. This one may too.

What makes it worth watching anyway is the pattern forming alongside the Sol result: two frontier AI systems, within one week, reportedly engaging with serious open problems and producing non-trivial outputs that at least one expert found worth announcing publicly.

If even one of these results survives verification, it changes how theorem-proving and formal proof workflows get designed — researchers will start treating frontier models as genuine mathematical collaborators, not just literature search tools.

For now, treat this as a claim under active scrutiny, not a confirmed breakthrough. The mathematics will decide, and that process takes time.

For builders, the practical takeaway is straightforward — Kimi K3 is worth evaluating when capacity reopens, but verify performance on your own tasks rather than trusting any lab's self-reported numbers.

Policy & Regulation Watch: Trump administration reportedly building a sanctions-based soft ban on Chinese AI models

According to a single report from The Decoder, the Trump administration is constructing what amounts to a slow-motion deterrent against US companies adopting Chinese AI models — not through an outright ban, but through sanctions lists and liability rules that make the legal and commercial risk prohibitive.

The mechanism matters: by placing Chinese AI providers on or near sanctions frameworks, the administration can make American companies nervous about using those models without ever passing explicit legislation or publishing formal rules.

No formal measures have been published yet. The report describes deliberations, not decisions, so the timeline and ultimate scope are genuinely uncertain.

But the direction is clear enough to flag for any developer or enterprise currently evaluating Kimi K3, Qwen, or similar models for production deployment. If these rules materialize, the question won't just be whether a model performs well — it'll be whether your legal team will sign off on using it.

The soft-ban approach is strategically clever and democratically murky in equal measure: it achieves policy goals while avoiding the scrutiny that formal rulemaking would invite.

One source, no published rules — treat this as a credible signal to monitor, not a policy to plan around just yet.

Research Breakthrough: AI advice makes people less accurate but more confident, study finds

A study reported by The Next Web finds that AI advice suppresses critical thinking — users who received AI-generated guidance ended up with less accurate answers but higher confidence in those answers than users who worked without AI assistance.

That combination is particularly problematic: worse outcomes paired with stronger certainty means people are less likely to catch or question their own errors.

The honest caveat is that available coverage doesn't detail the methodology, sample size, or task domains, so how broadly this generalizes is an open question.

But the directional finding aligns with what human factors researchers have documented in other automation contexts: when a system presents answers confidently, people anchor to them rather than scrutinizing them.

For anyone building AI decision-support tools in professional workflows — medical, legal, financial — this is a direct challenge to the premise that AI assistance reliably improves outcomes. Confident-sounding wrong answers may be worse than no answer at all.

One more finding worth keeping in that frame as we close out today's research news.
Source: https://thenextweb.com/news/ai-advice-suppresses-critical-thinking-wrong-answers-study

Research Breakthrough: LLMs generate novel hiring biases not present in human training data

New research covered by MIT Technology Review finds that large language models don't just inherit biases from human-generated training data — they develop their own novel biases in hiring contexts.

The distinction matters for compliance teams: if AI bias were purely inherited, you could theoretically audit and clean training data to address it. Novel emergent bias is harder to anticipate and harder to test for.

Available coverage is summary-level — the specific models tested, experimental conditions, and peer-review status aren't yet clear, so treat this as a preliminary finding rather than settled science.

Still, for enterprises running AI resume screening at scale, this is a direct compliance and fairness risk that warrants scrutiny before regulators ask the same questions.

The researchers' core point is that the bias problem in AI hiring tools is not just a data problem. It may be a model behavior problem, which requires a different class of solution entirely.
