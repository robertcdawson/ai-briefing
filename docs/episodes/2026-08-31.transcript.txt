Intro

For the last two years, generative AI has been transitioning from a software novelty into a foundational internet utility.

We are also tracking a sudden, massive procurement run on Apple hardware by the top AI labs.

Top Story: EU classifies ChatGPT as Very Large Online Search Engine under DSA

For anyone building consumer applications using OpenAI's infrastructure, the compliance environment just got significantly heavier.

The European Union has officially classified ChatGPT as a Very Large Online Search Engine under the Digital Services Act.

The trigger was ChatGPT crossing forty-five million monthly users in the EU.

By the end of 2026, OpenAI must deliver systemic risk assessments and maintain a public archive of all advertisements running on the platform.

The DSA strictly prohibits targeting ads based on religion, ethnicity, or political beliefs, and bans targeting minors entirely.

The classification puts ChatGPT under the same regulatory microscope as traditional tech giants, alongside Reddit and Roblox, which were just designated as Very Large Online Platforms.

The massive unknown is whether the European Commission can use this designation to demand access to OpenAI's training data.

Legal experts are actively fighting over whether the data access provisions apply to model weights and training corpora, leaving the ultimate cost of compliance unresolved.

Research Breakthrough: OpenAI and Anthropic buy tens of thousands of Mac minis to train computer-use agents

Training models to navigate graphical user interfaces requires an immense amount of realistic desktop environment data, and the leading labs are apparently brute-forcing that requirement.

One outlet reports that OpenAI purchased tens of thousands of Mac minis and Mac Studios specifically to train computer-use agents.

Anthropic is reportedly relying on the same Apple hardware for its own agent training.

This aligns with supply chain realities, as the most powerful Mac models have been sold out for months due to intense lab demand.

Apple's Mac revenue jumped nearly twenty-nine percent to ten-point-four billion dollars in the June quarter, and AI lab procurement appears to be a major driver.

Because this relies on a single report from The Information, the specific capability levels being trained and the expected release timelines remain unconfirmed.

But the scale of the hardware buy points to a near-term capability push aimed at completely reshaping how AI interacts with standard software.

That push requires capital, which brings us to OpenAI's revenue engine.

AI Business Watch: ChatGPT ad business hits one billion dollar annualized revenue run rate

The math on scaling a consumer AI product to hundreds of millions of users has never quite worked on subscription fees alone, which explains why OpenAI is aggressively standing up an advertising business.

OpenAI says its ChatGPT ad platform reached a one billion dollar annualized revenue run rate.

The company presents the global expansion of these ads as a mechanism to support broader free and affordable AI access.

Hitting a billion-dollar run rate signals that AI interfaces are beginning to compete directly with traditional search and social platforms for digital marketing budgets.

This figure is self-reported and annualized from current momentum, not audited historical revenue, and the actual pace of growth and profit margins remain undisclosed.

It shows OpenAI diversifying its income streams just as it experiments with entirely new ways to charge enterprise clients.

Before we get to that enterprise shift, there is a hardware development out of China.

Research Breakthrough: China's CXMT produces first HBM3E chips, narrowing AI memory gap

It is easy to dismiss small-scale chip manufacturing milestones when the global market is dominated by entrenched giants, but domestic Chinese production of AI memory changes the long-term export control calculus.

ChangXin Memory Technologies reportedly produced its first HBM3E chips.

This is the high-bandwidth memory required to feed data to leading AI accelerators like Nvidia's H100 and H200.

Right now, production is strictly in small quantities, and there is no confirmed timeline for when CXMT might ramp up to volume manufacturing.

We also do not know if the yield or performance can match the industry standards set by Samsung and SK Hynix.

Even at a pilot scale, demonstrating the capability to manufacture HBM3E locally reduces China's reliance on foreign suppliers and signals a potential erosion of Western leverage over the AI hardware supply chain.

Meanwhile, the economics of deploying AI software are shifting just as fast as the hardware.

AI Business Watch: OpenAI pilots outcome-based pricing charging customers only on task success

For the past three years, the enterprise AI market operated on a simple transaction where you paid for access — fixed subscription fees or per-query token charges — regardless of whether the model actually solved your problem.

Now, the industry is abandoning that model.

OpenAI is currently piloting an outcome-based pricing structure where it charges certain large customers only when the AI successfully completes a task.

This mirrors a broader enterprise software shift, with companies like Salesforce and Adobe also moving away from flat subscription fees for AI features.

Shifting to pay-on-success fundamentally alters the economics of deployment, aligning the vendor's revenue directly with the model's reliability.

The unresolved friction is attribution: when an AI assists a human rather than completing a task entirely autonomously, the dispute over who gets credit becomes a very real accounting problem.

This pricing pilot is limited to unnamed large clients for now.

Pricing software is one way to capture value; funding the underlying silicon ecosystem is another.
Source: https://the-decoder.com/openai-starts-charging-some-customers-only-when-its-ai-actually-works/

AI Business Watch: Nvidia invests 3.5 billion dollars in MediaTek to counter Big Tech custom chips

When hyperscalers like Google, Amazon, and Microsoft pour capital into building their own proprietary AI silicon, the dominant GPU supplier has to find new avenues to embed itself in the infrastructure stack.

Nvidia is addressing that threat by investing three-point-five billion dollars into Taiwanese chipmaker MediaTek.

MediaTek primarily supplies chips for edge devices like smartphones and consumer electronics.

By funding a major player in that space, Nvidia expands its reach beyond the data center, ensuring its architecture remains relevant as inference moves to local devices.

This strategic rationale is largely analyst interpretation, as the actual deal terms, equity stakes, and specific joint product roadmaps have not been disclosed.

The investment guarantees that as the major cloud providers try to bypass Nvidia in the server rack, Nvidia is busy securing its footprint everywhere else.
