Top Story: Anthropic researcher quits warning self-improving AI could kill humanity

Why does a pretraining researcher at a frontier lab suddenly resign to go on CNN and Fox News and demand his own industry slow down?

Jacob Coxon left Anthropic because he thinks the timeline to self-improving AI is compressing too fast, and he is calling for pacing agreements between labs to throttle development.

The Ars Technica headline captured the internal sentiment with a quote: 'We really do earnestly believe AI could kill all humans!'

For builders, this signals a fracturing consensus inside the frontier labs.

Extinction estimates are highly speculative, and Anthropic has not responded to Coxon's specific claims.

That tension over how labs operate internally is also spilling over into how they source their training data.

Research Breakthrough: Second mathematician accuses OpenAI of stealing unpublished math work

A second mathematician accusing OpenAI of stealing unpublished work might look like academic jealousy over getting beaten to a proof — but the timeline around the Navier-Stokes breakthrough is getting more complicated.

Andreas Thom posted concerns on Mastodon about highly specific ChatGPT interactions before OpenAI even announced their Millennium Prize claim.

He is calling their data practices unethical and dishonest, and The Verge is reporting allegations of scooping and spying.

OpenAI still has not detailed which mathematical datasets trained these models, though they deny any wrongdoing.

Both accusations rely on suspicious model outputs rather than direct proof of data ingestion.

While OpenAI defends its data pipeline, it is quietly making moves to shore up its safety credentials.

Policy & Regulation Watch: AI alignment pioneer Paul Christiano joins OpenAI Foundation board

Days ago, OpenAI faced a severe controversy over the recurrent-depth safety of its Astra model — and they needed technical credibility fast.

So they appointed Paul Christiano, the founder of the Alignment Research Center, to the OpenAI Foundation Board and its Safety and Security Committee.

TechCrunch called him a 'prominent AI doomer,' but for researchers, he is simply one of the most technically rigorous alignment voices globally.

The stated goal is to focus on AI alignment, safety, and standards.

What remains unclear is whether the Safety and Security Committee actually holds any operational authority to veto product decisions.

Governance is one way to handle risk; writing massive checks to copyright holders is another.

AI Business Watch: Suno launches v6 music model trained on licensed Warner, BMG, Believe data

Look at who is getting paid to understand Suno's latest release.

Suno is actively fighting copyright lawsuits from Universal Music and Sony Music, so for the launch of their v6 model, they licensed training data directly from Warner Music Group, BMG, and Believe.

They are rolling out three variants, including a free v6-mini, and aggressively shutting down all older models.

CEO Jack Brody claims the system was trained from the ground up with a new set of data — an attempt to establish a clean-slate copyright baseline to survive litigation.

We do not know which specific tracks were licensed, or if v6 is entirely free of the disputed content that Universal and Sony are suing over.

While generative audio companies spend heavily on data, enterprise customers are tightening their belts on compute.

AI Business Watch: Top AI spenders cut per-employee costs nearly 10 percent in August

If you route inference for an enterprise application, you are probably swapping out expensive frontier models for cheaper variants right now.

That optimization just showed up in the macroeconomic data.

According to the Ramp AI Index, AI spend per employee dropped nearly 10 percent in August among the top one percent of US corporate spenders.

The price per million tokens has plunged 41 percent since March.

Companies are actively substituting cheaper models, which complicates the growth forecasts for hyperscalers relying on massive compute margins.

This data only covers the heaviest enterprise spenders, but it suggests OpenAI and Anthropic will need massive volume increases to offset price compression.

That pressure to find widespread consumer volume brings us to Apple's latest hardware.

Product & Tool Watch: Apple launches foldable iPhone Duo with ambient AI listening and photo authentication

Apple is betting its hardware margins on the premise that consumers want AI features without cloud privacy risks.

At their fall event, they announced the iPhone Duo, their first foldable phone, alongside the Apple Watch Series 12.

The watch uses an S11 chip with a Secure Exclave to process ambient audio, ensuring raw audio is never saved as a file.

The iPhone 18 Pro introduces a Reference Image feature that cryptographically signs every pixel to detect AI manipulation.

CEO John Ternus declared the best AI device is still the iPhone.

The privacy claims rely entirely on hardware isolation, but independent security audits of this specific Secure Exclave implementation have not been published yet.
