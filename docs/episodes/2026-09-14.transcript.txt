Intro

"Whoever wins AI, wins."

That was the geopolitical veto delivered by Donald Trump this morning, September 14th, 2026, aimed squarely at a sudden, coordinated push by Silicon Valley's top executives to slow down their own industry.

We are looking at a fundamental fracture between the people building frontier models and the people regulating them.

Top Story: Amodei's AI slowdown call splits industry leaders and White House

The detail that fails to add up is why Sam Altman, Elon Musk, and Demis Hassabis are suddenly using X to publicly agree with Anthropic's Dario Amodei about hitting the brakes.

Last week, I said public defections over extinction timelines would force frontier labs into a corner, and that happened faster than expected.

Amodei published an open letter calling to pace the frontier, and the CEOs of the world's most fiercely competitive labs fell in line to support it.

Behind the scenes, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google have reportedly spent months discussing joint self-regulation.

But the mystery of how they actually enforce a pause collided immediately with Washington.

Trump opposed any slowdown that might let China gain ground, framing AI dominance as a winner-take-all race.

David Sacks echoed the resistance, arguing builders do not need regulations to pace themselves.

The pacing proposal lacks any defined mechanism, scope, or enforcement — and Altman is simultaneously promising rapid progress will continue, leaving researchers to guess whether these talks produce binding commitments or just buy time.

Policy & Regulation Watch: Anthropic confirms Houthis used Claude Code

The immediate reaction to hearing that a sanctioned armed group used an AI coding assistant is to assume it is just another panicked report about someone asking a chatbot for public physics equations.

But a new report claims the Houthis used Claude Code specifically to develop missile guidance software, and that Anthropic itself disclosed the misuse.

On Friday, I noted that Anthropic publicly naming competitors for distillation marked the end of the quiet era of threat intelligence.

Now they are allegedly tracking kinetic weapons development on their own infrastructure, which forces a massive conversation about API access verification and export controls.

Because this comes from a single outlet called Clash Report and Anthropic's statement has not been independently verified, the exact method of access remains opaque.

Product & Tool Watch: Perplexity deploys Astra autonomously

Ten days ago, OpenAI launched Astra. Last week, they shipped the Agents API. Today, Perplexity has handed the model the keys to its live production systems — writing communications, changing software, and monitoring infrastructure end-to-end.

When the model launched, I called the AGI marketing push noise, but said its reasoning performance would force infrastructure changes.

Perplexity is proving that out, reporting that the agent checks in far less frequently than previous generations.

This is one of the first major examples of a frontier model operating autonomously in a critical environment.

We only know this from an OpenAI promotional case study, however — no external reliability metrics, incident rates, or details on human oversight have been disclosed.
Source: https://openai.com/index/perplexity-improving-accuracy-with-astra

Open Source Watch: Garry Tan urges US labs to distill models

Training a frontier model costs billions in compute, but extracting its capabilities costs a fraction of that, which dictates exactly how the open-weight ecosystem is reacting to recent threats.

Friday's Anthropic report framed model distillation as a hostile attack by Chinese firms.

Today, Y Combinator president Garry Tan published a call for US open-weight labs to aggressively distill those same frontier models themselves.

Tan is reframing capability extraction as a legitimate competitive strategy rather than a security breach, which complicates the export-control argument and pushes capability diffusion faster.

Tan's post generated massive interest on Hacker News, though it is strictly a public opinion piece right now, lacking any technical roadmap or named lab partners.
Source: https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/11/y-combinators-garry-tan-wants-u-s-open-weight-ai-labs-to-distill-frontier-models-too/

Product & Tool Watch: ElevenLabs releases Music v2.5

If you are integrating audio generation into an application this morning, you now have a second major provider offering an API backed entirely by licensed training data.

ElevenLabs just released Music v2.5, explicitly stating the model was trained exclusively on licensed tracks.

This comes right after Suno's v6 launch last week, turning licensed data from a legal shield into a baseline product requirement.

ElevenLabs claims a blind test of nearly forty-eight thousand comparison pairs showed listeners preferred the new version over its predecessor, and they are offering it via app and API with both free and pro tiers.

The quality claim comes entirely from the company's internal testing, with no external audio evaluation published yet.
Source: https://the-decoder.com/elevenlabs-makes-music-v2-5-available-via-app-and-api-with-free-and-pro-tier-options/

Open Source Watch: AllSpark releases Iris search agents

Open-weight search agents are closing the performance gap with proprietary systems, assuming self-reported benchmarks hold up in the wild.

The AllSpark team released Iris-mini and Iris-pro, built on Qwen models, which now lead their size class for search agent tasks.

The interesting technical detail is that their training data improvements also boosted performance on general tool use and office tasks that were not explicitly in the training set.

They published both the models and the training data as open source.

The strongest-in-class claim relies entirely on the team's own paper and has not been reproduced externally.
Source: https://the-decoder.com/iris-mini-and-iris-pro-are-the-strongest-open-weight-search-agents-in-their-class/
