Intro

One dollar and thirty-eight cents per hour. That's what Google DeepMind is charging for a real-time voice API that, as of today, sits at the top of the Artificial Analysis speech-to-speech leaderboard.

That number is the story. September 16th, and the live voice market just got a lot more competitive.

Top Story: Google DeepMind launches Gemini 3.8 Live voice models undercutting OpenAI pricing

Here's the detail that doesn't quite add up: Google DeepMind launched Gemini 3.8 Live today, it leads the Artificial Analysis speech-to-speech benchmark, and it's priced at a dollar thirty-eight per hour. Benchmark leader, cheaper than the competition. That combination usually means one of two things — either the benchmark is measuring the wrong thing, or OpenAI has a real problem.

The answer is probably some of both. Three independent sources confirm the pricing and the leaderboard position, so those facts are solid. What the Artificial Analysis benchmark doesn't fully capture is naturalness — and that's where OpenAI's GPT-Live-1 reportedly still has an edge, because it uses full-duplex audio architecture. Full-duplex means the model can listen and speak simultaneously, the way humans actually talk, rather than taking turns. That produces more natural-feeling conversation, and it's not something a benchmark score necessarily reflects.

Google also dropped a second model simultaneously: Gemini 3.8 Live Extended Thinking. That variant adds a reasoning layer to real-time audio — the model can work through a problem while talking to you, not just retrieve and respond. That combination hasn't existed at this price point before, and it opens up use cases that were previously too expensive to run at scale: live tutoring, complex customer triage, anything where the voice interface needs to think rather than just pattern-match.

If you're optimizing for cost and benchmark position, Google just moved to the front. If you're optimizing for how natural the conversation feels in production, OpenAI's architecture may still win the user preference test even if it loses the leaderboard. Developers building voice products right now have a real decision to make, and the right answer depends on which dimension their users will actually notice.

The enterprise reasoning angle connects to something Salesforce and Nvidia just announced — a domain-specific model that makes a similar bet on open-weight foundations.

Product & Tool Watch: Salesforce and Nvidia release enterprise reasoning model built on open-weight Nemotron

The pushback writes itself: Salesforce has been building CRM software for twenty-five years, Nvidia makes chips — why should anyone take their jointly released AI model seriously when Anthropic and OpenAI exist?

Here's the answer. Salesforce Koa is built on Nvidia's open-weight Nemotron model and fine-tuned specifically for sales, marketing, and customer support. The claim isn't that it beats GPT-5 on a general reasoning benchmark. The claim is that for the narrow set of tasks Salesforce customers actually run — drafting follow-up emails, triaging support tickets, scoring leads — a domain-specific model trained on that exact distribution of work can match or exceed a frontier generalist at a fraction of the cost.

That's a testable thesis, and it matters structurally. Stratechery frames this as Salesforce abandoning its traditional moat — the UI, the dashboards, the workflow screens — in favor of headless agent interfaces that just do the work without a human clicking through menus. If agents become the interface, then the software layer that used to lock customers in becomes irrelevant, and what matters is whether the model is good enough at the job.

No independent benchmark comparisons against frontier models have been published. Two sources cover this, but neither includes head-to-head evaluation data, and pricing and API availability weren't disclosed at launch. So right now this is a credible architectural argument with no public proof of performance. Watch for the evals.

The political environment those models will be deployed into is getting more complicated — the infrastructure they run on is facing serious public resistance.

Policy & Regulation Watch: NYT-Siena poll finds 61 percent of likely voters oppose AI data center construction

Start the timeline in 2022. The AI infrastructure buildout begins in earnest, and the dominant political narrative is that the US needs to win the compute race. Congress is broadly supportive. The energy and land-use implications are treated as engineering problems, not political ones.

Fast-forward to 2024 and 2025. Data centers start landing in specific neighborhoods — not abstract policy, but actual construction near homes, near defunct industrial sites, near power grids that communities depend on. Philadelphia officials proposed building one near a defunct oil refinery. The scale becomes visible: US data centers are now projected to consume more natural gas than Germany and Japan combined by 2035.

Which brings you to today. A New York Times and Siena University poll of fifteen hundred and three likely voters, conducted in early September, finds sixty-one percent opposed to data center construction and only fourteen percent strongly in support. The number that should stop infrastructure planners cold is the partisan breakdown: even 2024 Trump voters split nearly evenly, forty-nine percent in favor versus forty-five percent opposed. This isn't a left-right issue anymore.

Three independent sources confirm the poll and its context, so the numbers are solid. What's less clear is causation — the survey doesn't separate energy and land-use concerns from AI-specific anxiety, and it was conducted before the recent AI safety debate intensified. But for permitting, siting, and energy policy, that distinction may not matter. A sixty-one percent opposition number is a concrete political constraint regardless of what's driving it.

The business fallout from that political environment is already showing up in how enterprise customers are treating the AI companies that need that infrastructure.

AI Business Watch: Hugging Face bills OpenAI $100 million after alleged compute infrastructure hack

Follow the money first. Hugging Face CEO Clément Delangue is demanding a hundred million dollars from OpenAI, claiming compute traces link OpenAI to unauthorized access to Hugging Face infrastructure. If Delangue collects, Hugging Face gets a hundred million dollars and a massive public narrative win over its largest competitor in the developer ecosystem. If the claim doesn't hold up, OpenAI absorbs the reputational cost of having to publicly deny it.

The incentive structure here should make you cautious. This story comes from a single source — The Next Web — with no independent confirmation, no disclosed details on how the compute traces were attributed to OpenAI specifically, and no public response from OpenAI as of reporting. A hundred million dollars is a number designed to generate coverage, and it did: a hundred and forty-five upvotes on Hacker News. That's not corroboration.

What would make this story real: a second source confirming the technical attribution, details on the forensic methodology, or evidence that legal action has been filed. None of those exist yet. Treat this as unverified and attributed to a single outlet until something changes.

Enterprise data trust is where the story gets substantive, and that's a thread we've been tracking — it connects directly to what happened with Anthropic's Fable model.

AI Business Watch: Palantir, Nvidia, Booz Allen pull back from Anthropic Fable over 30-day log retention

Monday morning, practically speaking: if you're deploying Anthropic's Fable model for a client in defense, intelligence, or any regulated industry, you now have three named organizations — Palantir, Nvidia, and Booz Allen Hamilton — who've reduced their usage specifically because Anthropic stores usage logs for thirty days. That's the policy. Thirty days of logs is thirty days of sensitive query history sitting in someone else's infrastructure.

Yesterday we covered leaked documents from OpenAI's Project Lily showing contractors reading ChatGPT conversations — and I said then that default-on data sharing was a massive liability for enterprise deployment. That call is holding up. What Fable demonstrates is that the liability doesn't require a leak. The policy itself, disclosed and acknowledged, is enough to trigger a pullback from major customers.

Both OpenAI and Anthropic tell corporate customers their data won't be used for training. The Decoder's framing is that no policy statement has actually closed the trust gap, and the Fable pullback is the evidence. The distinction customers are drawing isn't between training and not-training — it's about log retention duration and access controls, which are separate questions that the standard enterprise agreements haven't resolved.

The Decoder doesn't specify the scale of the pullback. These organizations may have stopped using Fable for specific sensitive workloads while continuing to use it elsewhere — that's a meaningful difference from a full exit, and we don't know which it is.

The cost pressure on frontier labs from that trust gap is compounding — and open models are increasingly positioned to absorb the customers who decide the risk isn't worth it.

Research Breakthrough: Mozilla report finds open Chinese models close frontier gap with four-month lag

The number that matters here is four months. Mozilla's forthcoming report, previewed exclusively by Ars Technica, finds that open Chinese models trail frontier closed models by approximately four months on capability benchmarks — and cost roughly one-fifth the price per token.

That's a usable framework. A four-month lag means that for most production use cases that don't require the absolute frontier — customer support, document summarization, code completion at standard complexity — an open model is probably good enough today. And at one-fifth the cost, the build-versus-buy calculation tips hard toward open for anyone running at scale.

The specific focus on Chinese open models matters because it maps to the geopolitical argument that export controls and compute restrictions would slow Chinese AI development. A four-month capability lag, if it holds, suggests the gap is narrow enough that restrictions haven't produced a meaningful divergence — at least on the open model side.

The methodology caveat is real: Mozilla hasn't fully disclosed which model pairs were compared or how the capability gap was measured. Benchmark gaps vary enormously by task domain. A four-month lag on a coding benchmark and a four-month lag on scientific reasoning are very different claims. Ars Technica got an exclusive preview, which means independent replication of the methodology hasn't happened yet.

But even with those caveats, the directional finding is consistent with what developers have been observing in practice. The cost differential alone is enough to drive adoption decisions. The question for closed frontier labs isn't whether open models will eventually close the gap — it's whether four months is enough runway to stay commercially relevant.

If you're making infrastructure or model selection decisions in the next quarter, the Mozilla report is worth reading in full when it drops.
