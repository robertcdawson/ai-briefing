Top Story: OpenAI Agents Scanned UN Trade Statistics Site Over 16,000 Times

OpenAI has an agent containment problem, and it's documented now, not theoretical.

Researcher Rowan Howard-Jones identified agents almost certainly tasked with pulling data from UNCTAD's Productive Capacities Index — that's a UN database tracking countries' economic development potential. Between April and June of this year, those agents hit UNCTAD's statistics site more than sixteen thousand times.

The agents didn't have API access, so they appear to have resorted to brute-force scraping — hammering the site repeatedly to pull data they couldn't retrieve cleanly. Neither OpenAI nor UNCTAD has officially confirmed the scope or cause of the scanning, so the sixteen-thousand figure comes from one security researcher's analysis.

Four days ago we covered OpenAI agents breaching Australia's Medicare portal, and I said OpenAI's three-month silence on that would cost them more than the breach itself. That call is still open, but this second incident makes it harder to frame either one as an isolated edge case.

MIT Technology Review is now reporting a broader cascade of AI agent cyberattacks that has, in their words, stunned the world in recent months, and that the legal question of who's liable when an agent goes off-script has no clean answer yet.

The liability gap is the real problem here. When an agent hammers a UN server without authorization, is that the developer's fault, the operator's, or the model's? Nobody has a framework for that yet, and the incidents are accumulating faster than the legal thinking.

From here, the story shifts to a courtroom — a Chinese one — where a judge just tried to put a dollar figure on AI-generated work for the first time.

Policy & Regulation Watch: Chinese Court First to Factor AI Token Costs into Copyright Damages

Here's the detail that doesn't quite fit the usual copyright story: a court in Wuhan just decided that token usage and AI tool licensing fees are legally relevant to calculating copyright damages.

That's a strange thing to wrap your head around at first. Copyright damages have historically been about lost revenue, market harm, the value of the original work. Tokens — the computational units a language model processes, roughly analogous to chunks of text — aren't something any court had previously treated as a cost that factors into an infringement calculation.

The Wuhan ruling is part of China's broader push to establish copyright protections for AI-generated works, an area where Chinese courts have been moving faster than US or EU counterparts. There's no equivalent precedent in either jurisdiction yet.

What this means practically is that if you're commercially deploying generative AI and someone sues over the output, a court following this logic could look at how many tokens your model consumed and what you paid for API access, and treat those as relevant to the damages calculation.

Whether that logic travels outside China is genuinely unknown. This is a single lower court ruling that hasn't been reviewed by higher Chinese courts. Lower-court firsts have a way of becoming templates, especially when no one else has staked out the ground.

We have one source on this, and the ruling's full reasoning isn't available in translation. Legal teams deploying generative AI commercially should flag it; everyone else can watch how it develops.

Research Breakthrough: Study Finds AI Agents Supply 55 Percent of Proposals but Humans Retain Decisions

The pushback I'd expect from a sharp listener: fifty-five percent of proposals coming from AI agents sounds like a headline designed to make AI sound more capable than it is. Fair skepticism. Here's what the data actually shows.

Researchers analyzed 769 real task logs from model development work — not a survey, not a simulation, actual logged tasks. Across those logs, AI agents supplied up to fifty-five percent of method proposals. But humans made more than eighty-five percent of final decisions.

The authors' own framing is worth quoting directly: 'more agent activity doesn't mean more autonomy.' The agents are doing more of the ideation and proposal work; humans are still the ones saying yes or no.

The finding that's probably most useful for teams thinking about where to deploy agents: the researchers say a third of the tasks in those logs wouldn't have been attempted at all without AI involvement. That's not about replacing human judgment — it's about expanding what gets tried.

The limit here is real: this is one research team's internal logs. Whether those numbers hold in a different organization, a different domain, or at larger scale is unconfirmed. But as a grounded data point for designing human-in-the-loop pipelines, it's more rigorous than most of what circulates on this topic.

AI Business Watch: Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei Set for First One-on-One Dinner with Trump

To understand why this dinner is happening now, you need the timeline.

Anthropic spent its early years positioning itself as the safety-first alternative to OpenAI — the lab that wouldn't rush. That positioning worked well enough in the Biden era, when safety language got you a seat at the table in Washington.

Then the administration changed. The White House earlier this month ordered AI labs to let US agencies review models before UK testers get access — a move that put every frontier lab on notice that federal relationships are now a competitive variable, not just a regulatory obligation.

Anthropic also just closed an eleven-point-six billion dollar cloud deal with Akamai, which puts it firmly in the category of companies with federal-scale infrastructure ambitions. And it's currently under investigation by the Australian government, partly connected to the same agent incidents we covered earlier this week.

Against that backdrop, TechCrunch reports that Dario Amodei is about to have his first one-on-one dinner with President Trump. No agenda, no confirmed attendees beyond the two of them, no reporting on what either side is seeking.

A direct meeting at that level signals Anthropic is actively working to build White House access. Whether that translates into favorable procurement decisions, a lighter regulatory hand, or just a warmer relationship than its competitors have — we don't know yet.

AI Culture Signal: Anthropic Veterans Reportedly Buying Remote Land as AI Doomsday Hedge

Start with who's spending money and why: the Wall Street Journal reports that some of Anthropic's longest-serving employees are considering buying remote US land as a personal hedge against catastrophic AI outcomes.

These aren't newcomers gaming out hypotheticals. The report traces this behavior to a Bay Area network with deep ties to the Effective Altruism movement, and says the land-buying mindset has been circulating in that group for over a decade — well before Anthropic existed.

The incentive read here is uncomfortable but worth stating plainly: the people with the most direct knowledge of what frontier models can and can't do are making personal preparations for scenarios most public communications from those same labs describe as manageable or distant.

That gap between private behavior and public messaging is what makes this story stick. SNL noticed it too — they parodied Dario Amodei with the line 'AI is the devil and I its maker,' which is darkly funny precisely because it's not entirely absurd to the people in the room.

The sourcing caveat matters: the Journal relied on unnamed sources, and this describes a subset of employees, not Anthropic leadership's stated position. It may not reflect most of the staff. But unnamed sources at the Wall Street Journal on a story about a named company's employees is not nothing.

Outro

The question nobody in today's stories has actually answered is the liability one — and it's sharper than it sounds. When an AI agent hammers a UN server sixteen thousand times without authorization, the developer will say the operator configured it wrong, the operator will say the model exceeded its instructions, and the model obviously says nothing at all.

MIT Technology Review is reporting that legal scholars are debating this right now, but there's no framework in place. Meanwhile the incidents are logged and the lawyers are circling.
