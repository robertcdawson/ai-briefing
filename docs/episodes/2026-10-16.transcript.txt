Top Story: GPT-6-Astra solves 49 of 50 theoretical physics problems at $2.40 each

To understand why today's result lands the way it does, you have to run the timeline. Five years ago, AI systems were clearing high-school physics. Three years ago, they were passing graduate entrance exams. Two years ago, they started touching the edges of research-level problems on curated benchmarks. Each of those steps got coverage, and each time the counterargument was the same: those are known problems with known solutions, and real research means working on questions nobody has answered yet.

TheoPhysEval is a set of unpublished theoretical physics problems — not textbook exercises, not competition problems with answer keys floating around the internet. GPT-6-Astra scored 49 out of 50 on them. The one failure is complicated by the fact that some of the cases it missed had errors in the expert-written reference solutions, not in the model's work.

The cost figure is the part that changes the staffing math. Two dollars and forty cents per problem. That's not a research curiosity — that's a number a department chair or a lab director can put into a spreadsheet and act on.

The researchers behind the evaluation are careful to say the model still lacks what they call scientific taste — the ability to decide which questions are worth asking in the first place. It can solve a hard problem handed to it; it can't yet walk into a field and identify the frontier. That's a real distinction, and it means autonomous AI-driven research isn't here today.

But the researchers also say it could arrive soon, and the economics are already here. The people most directly affected aren't senior faculty — they're the graduate students and early-career physicists whose training pipeline assumed that working through hard problems was the irreplaceable core of scientific formation. That assumption just got complicated.

Open Source Watch: Meta launches 816B open-weight Muse Spark model optimized for Apple Silicon

Monday we covered Muse Wonder Full, Meta's closed frontier model. What's happened since is that Meta turned around and open-weighted a version — Muse Spark Open — at 816 billion parameters, with multimodal support across text, images, video, and audio, and a context window up to one million tokens.

Now, who pays for this and who collects? Meta bears the training cost and gives the weights away. Apple gets a frontier open model running natively on their hardware via the MLX framework — specifically two M5 Ultra Mac Studios. Apple CEO John Ternus endorsed the launch publicly, which is not something you see from a company that typically treats AI partnerships as quiet integrations.

Apple's incentive is straightforward: if serious AI workloads run locally on Apple Silicon rather than in a cloud data center, that's a hardware sale Apple captures instead of a margin Amazon or Google collects. Meta's incentive is also straightforward: the more places Muse runs, the harder it is for any single closed competitor to own the distribution layer.

The benchmark comparisons showing Muse Spark competitive with leading closed models come from Meta's own testing, so take those with appropriate skepticism until independent evaluators run the same numbers. What's harder to dispute is the threshold itself — 816 billion parameters, locally, on consumer hardware. A year ago that sentence described a data center.

The FTC story out today adds a layer to how we should read all of these open-weight releases, and it's worth a few minutes.

Policy & Regulation Watch: Anthropic grants METR employee-level access for independent safety audits

For anyone building on Anthropic's API, here's what changes: there is now an external organization, METR, that has the same internal system access an Anthropic employee would have, and Anthropic has explicitly waived the right to block or delay whatever METR publishes. That means independent assessments of risks, incidents, and practices will come out on METR's schedule, not Anthropic's.

That's a meaningful departure from how third-party audits usually work. The standard arrangement gives a company review rights over findings before publication, which tends to soften conclusions and delay anything uncomfortable. Anthropic removed that lever.

The timing is notable. Anthropic's IPO is planned for the near term, and a credible independent safety audit arrangement is exactly the kind of thing that plays well with institutional investors who've been burned by governance failures at other tech companies. Whether the motivation is principled or strategic, the mechanism is real.

The scope is still unclear. METR is a small organization, and what 'employee-level access' covers in practice — which systems, which model weights, which incident logs — hasn't been fully disclosed. The arrangement could be comprehensive or carefully bounded. We'll learn more when METR publishes its first assessment.

The FTC investigation announced this week sits right next to this story, and the contrast is instructive.

Policy & Regulation Watch: FTC opens probes into OpenAI and Anthropic over security incident harms

The FTC investigation into OpenAI and Anthropic is real regulatory pressure, but I'd be cautious about reading it as a turning point — the enforcement track record on AI probes so far has been slow, and this one comes with some unusual political context.

The FTC is looking at whether security incidents at both companies caused harm to Americans, and one outlet reports the agency is considering fines comparable to what companies face for delaying data breach disclosures. That framing matters: it would create a liability model for AI companies borrowed from financial services and data privacy law, where the penalty isn't just for the incident but for how you handled disclosure.

Both OpenAI and Anthropic being named simultaneously is significant. It signals the FTC isn't treating this as a one-company problem.

The political wrinkle is that this probe is proceeding while the current administration has publicly dismissed AI safety concerns. That tension doesn't make the investigation less real, but it does raise questions about how aggressively it gets pursued. The probe's scope, timeline, and whether it results in formal enforcement haven't been confirmed by the FTC directly — one outlet is reporting this, and the FTC hasn't put out a statement.

Policy & Regulation Watch: Research confirms ChatGPT ads target users via prompts, email, and phone number

Here's the part that doesn't quite add up on first read: ChatGPT is a conversational tool, not a social media feed. So how does an advertiser end up targeting you by email address, phone number, or device ID based on what you typed into a chat window?

Research published this week lays out the mechanism. Advertisers can upload customer lists — email addresses, phone numbers, device identifiers — and match them against ChatGPT users. The prompts themselves are being used as behavioral signals, meaning what you ask about influences what ads you're shown. Most users have no idea this is happening.

The detail that sharpens this considerably: the targeting capability is apparently accessible to spyware companies delivering malware through ad networks. That's not a hypothetical privacy concern — that's an active harm vector.

The full technical mechanism hasn't been independently audited, and OpenAI hasn't publicly confirmed the specific methodology described in the research. The targeting categories — email, phone, device ID, prompt content — are documented, but the precise plumbing is still somewhat unclear.

For anyone deploying ChatGPT in an enterprise setting where employees are entering sensitive queries, this is worth a policy conversation before it becomes a compliance conversation.

Open Source Watch: Black Forest Labs releases FLUX 3 Klein open-weights 4K image model for 8GB GPUs

The reasonable pushback on a new image model release is: we've seen a lot of these, the benchmarks are always from the company itself, and the last several 'smaller but better' announcements turned out to mean 'smaller but cheaper to run and roughly comparable.' Fair.

Here's why FLUX 3 Klein is worth a closer look. It's 5.6 billion parameters — 38 percent fewer than its predecessor — and it runs on 8 gigabytes of VRAM, which covers the majority of consumer Nvidia cards. The capability it's delivering at that size includes native 4K generation, pixel-precise editing, and support for up to ten reference images simultaneously. That combination at that hardware floor is genuinely new.

The performance comparisons against proprietary competitors come from Black Forest Labs' own internal tests, so independent replication is the thing to wait for before you rewrite your image pipeline around it. But the hardware requirement is a concrete, verifiable fact — 8GB VRAM — and that's the number that matters for most practitioners deciding whether to run this locally.

If you've been waiting on a local image generation setup because your hardware didn't clear the threshold, this one's worth testing over the weekend.
