Intro

Nick Cammarata published a 26,000-word blog post on October 20th, 2026, and opened it with the line 'I think I have good news about the nature of neural networks' — and somehow that understated opener touched off what MIT Tech Review called 'wild speculation as well as cautious, and not-so-cautious, celebration' across the field.

Let's get into what he's actually claiming, and whether the reaction is warranted.

Top Story: Goodfire researcher's 26,000-word post claims to upend neural network understanding

Here's the pushback a careful listener should have right away: this is a blog post from a researcher at an AI startup, not a peer-reviewed paper, and the AI world has a long history of getting excited about mechanistic interpretability claims that don't replicate.

That's a fair objection. Hold onto it.

Nick Cammarata works at Goodfire, a company focused on AI interpretability — that's the field trying to understand what's actually happening inside a neural network, not just what it outputs. The core question has always been: are the features and circuits researchers identify in smaller models real structural properties of large networks, or are they artifacts of how we're looking?

Cammarata's post argues the former — that there's a coherent, generalizable structure to how neural networks represent information, and that researchers have been misreading it.

MIT Tech Review says the claim 'knocked the AI world on its head,' and the response has ranged from serious engagement to full-on celebration.

Now, back to that objection. It stands. The post hasn't been peer-reviewed, the findings haven't been independently replicated by outside labs, and Goodfire has a commercial interest in interpretability being tractable and important. None of that makes Cammarata wrong, but it means the field's enthusiasm is running ahead of the evidence right now.

If the core claim holds up under scrutiny, the downstream effects are real — better tools for debugging model behavior, more reliable capability prediction, and a firmer foundation for alignment research. Those are not small things.

For now, treat this as a high-signal hypothesis worth watching, not a settled result. The next thing to look for is whether labs outside Goodfire try to replicate or falsify the central finding.

Research Breakthrough: Single hacker used autonomous AI agents to breach over 100 major company websites

A quick line on where we've been: five days ago, we covered a rogue fleet of Chinese AI agents autonomously scraping the web, and I said that incident would accelerate enterprise security requirements for agentic deployments. That call is looking more right by the day, and here's why.

For years, the security research community warned that AI agents — systems that can browse, write code, and take actions in sequence without a human approving each step — would eventually be weaponized offensively. The rogue scraping fleet was a nuisance. This is a different order of magnitude.

AI security firm Norforge reports that a single hacker used AI agents to breach more than 100 websites. Confirmed victims include John Deere, SharkNinja, and Cummins, per Reuters. The attacks were discovered because the hacker left traces in Caido, an AI-assisted hacking tool.

One person, operating semi-autonomously through AI tooling, hit over a hundred targets. The labor constraint that used to limit the scale of attacks like this is gone.

What Norforge hasn't disclosed is the full victim list or exactly how much of the attack chain was autonomous versus human-directed. That distinction matters for how defenders respond — a fully autonomous attack requires a different mitigation strategy than one where a human is still steering each breach.

What's clear enough is that the threat model for enterprise security teams has to account for agentic attackers now, not just agentic defenders.

AI Business Watch: CoreWeave debt distress triggers neocloud shakeout with Nvidia most exposed

Start with who's holding the bag. CoreWeave's bonds are trading at more than 1,000 basis points over government bond yields — that's the threshold where debt is considered distressed, meaning investors are pricing in a real chance the company can't service what it owes. The stock fell more than 20 percent in a single week.

CoreWeave is one of the largest GPU cloud providers — companies that rent Nvidia hardware to AI developers who don't want to buy their own. They're called neoclouds, and they've been the infrastructure layer under a huge portion of AI development over the past few years.

Now follow the money upward. Nvidia sells chips. Neoclouds buy those chips on credit, rent them out, and need steady customers to cover the debt. Customers outside the big hyperscalers — Amazon, Microsoft, Google — account for 45 percent of Nvidia's data center revenue. If neoclouds start failing, that revenue base shrinks fast.

Microsoft already pulled out of a deal with Nscale for 23,000 Nvidia chips, which is a signal that even the hyperscalers are getting selective about who they commit capacity through.

Industry executives — unnamed ones, so take the precision loosely — expect only 7 to 10 of the hundreds of AI cloud providers currently operating to survive the shakeout.

For builders, that means vendor lock-in risk is real right now. If you're running production workloads on a neocloud that isn't in the top tier, it's worth asking whether your provider will be around in 18 months.

CoreWeave's distress may not be a perfect proxy for every neocloud's health — some have better customer concentration or lower leverage — but the direction of travel is clear.

Policy & Regulation Watch: Two German courts hold Google legally liable for false AI Overview statements

The German court rulings on Google's AI Overviews are more consequential than they look, and the reason is the legal theory, not just the outcome.

In the Munich case, Google lost, appealed, then quietly settled — which means there's no binding appellate ruling, but also means Google decided the case wasn't worth fighting to a higher court. Then the Aachen Regional Court applied the same reasoning in a separate case, where Google's AI falsely attributed criminal offenses to a doctor.

The legal theory both courts are using is that Google's AI-generated answers are the company's own content, not third-party output it's merely hosting. That framing matters enormously. It removes the intermediary liability shield that platforms have historically relied on when user-generated or third-party content causes harm.

Applied to AI search, it means every factual claim an AI Overview makes is potentially the publisher's legal liability. For a system that generates answers at scale across millions of queries, that's a structurally different risk profile.

These are German regional courts, not the EU Court of Justice, and the theory hasn't been tested in the UK, US, or most other major markets. But two courts reaching the same conclusion independently is a data point, and legal theories that win in lower courts have a way of traveling.

Product teams building AI-generated answer features anywhere in the EU should be watching whether this reasoning gets picked up at the appellate level.

The part that needs explaining is the cost. Half the price of the predecessor model, with meaningfully better performance. That's not the normal direction for capability improvements.

MiniMax hasn't fully disclosed the architectural changes that enabled this, so we're working from benchmark numbers and pricing announcements — both of which come primarily from MiniMax's own reporting and the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index. Real-world task performance may vary.

What the numbers do establish is a trend: Chinese open models keep compressing the price-performance curve faster than Western API providers are matching it. For developers choosing an inference provider, that compression is a real factor in product economics.

The thing to watch on this story is whether Western providers respond with pricing moves in the next few weeks, and whether independent evaluations of M3.1 Flash hold up the benchmark claims outside of controlled conditions.
