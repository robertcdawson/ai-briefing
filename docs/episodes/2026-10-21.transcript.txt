Top Story: Claude Science autonomously designs protein binders targeting deadly scorpion neurotoxins

We spent years watching AI predict how existing proteins fold, and then the models started suggesting minor tweaks to known molecules.

Today, Anthropic crossed into full autonomous generation.

Their Claude Science platform was given a target: Cn2, a deadly neurotoxin produced by the Centruroides noxius scorpion.

The AI generated over three hundred and eighteen thousand potential protein binders without human intervention.

Researchers took two hundred and eleven of those designs into a physical wet lab to see if the math held up in reality.

Seventeen met the strict validation criteria, and two bound extremely tightly to the toxin.

The implication for anyone working in computational biology is massive, because the model handled the grunt work of experimental design on its own.

Binding to a toxin in a petri dish is miles away from functioning as an antivenom in a living patient, and the results haven't faced formal scientific review yet.

Policy & Regulation Watch: US orders all federal civilian agencies to remove DeepSeek AI products immediately

Look at the procurement budgets for federal civilian agencies, and you will see why DeepSeek gained traction.

Their V4.1-Flash model offered severe cost and speed advantages over Western alternatives, making it an attractive line item for government IT contractors.

The Department of Homeland Security just cut off that revenue stream entirely by issuing an immediate removal order for all DeepSeek products across federal civilian agencies, citing national security concerns.

This mandate goes beyond banning the direct use of the model, explicitly covering any third-party services built using DeepSeek technology underneath.

Right now, this only applies to civilian federal agencies, so state governments and private enterprises are technically untouched.

Federal procurement rules usually act like a leading indicator for corporate compliance, meaning enterprise budgets will likely start shifting away from Chinese AI providers by the end of the quarter.

AI Business Watch: OpenAI acquires Orchestra Bio to accelerate AI-driven drug development pipeline

For engineers building orchestration tools for medical research, your competitive landscape just got a lot steeper.

OpenAI acquired Orchestra Bio, a startup focused on accelerating the ten to fifteen years it usually takes to get a new drug to market in the US.

Orchestra's team and technology are merging directly into Rosalind Workbench, which is OpenAI's experiment-planning software.

OpenAI already has major partnerships with pharma giants Novo Nordisk and Moderna.

By bringing Orchestra in-house, they are framing their platform as a way for pharma teams to autonomously coordinate clinical trials and spot statistical problems early.

The acquisition terms are private, and it remains to be seen if Orchestra's specific tech can actually shave years off a heavily regulated clinical trial process.

Product & Tool Watch: GPT-6 jumps from 38 to 87 percent on multi-language benchmark including COBOL

Last week, we talked about GPT-6-Astra solving theoretical physics problems, and I noted that autonomous research in the hard sciences was arriving faster than expected.

Today's update presents a strange contrast, because OpenAI is suddenly boasting about a language from the 1950s.

The company just announced that GPT-6 Astra scored eighty-seven point four percent on a new evaluation called MultiLangBench across twenty languages.

That is a massive leap from the roughly thirty-eight percent scored by GPT-5.6 Terra.

The real prize here is COBOL, where GPT-6.1 Sol hit ninety-seven point nine percent accuracy specifically on migration tasks.

OpenAI is framing this as a win for low-resource programming languages like Fortran and Visual Basic, but the financial incentive is obvious.

COBOL still runs critical banking and government mainframes that have resisted modernization for decades.

Passing a new, unverified benchmark is entirely different from safely migrating a live banking system.

Open Source Watch: DeepSeek open-sources DeepInfer inference engine behind its fastest cheapest model

You might look at DeepSeek open-sourcing its DeepInfer engine and think it hardly matters to anyone who doesn't run their own massive server farm.

But if you are renting API access from Western providers, this release directly impacts your bottom line.

By releasing the tools that drastically reduce compute costs and energy use at inference time, DeepSeek is handing the broader developer community a way to run models far cheaper.
