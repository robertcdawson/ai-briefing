Intro

Fifty domain experts sat down with an AI agent, worked through judgment calls, reached conclusions — and up to 86 percent of the time, they landed on whatever view the agent happened to hold, which was set by a random coin toss before the session started.

Most of them walked away believing they'd reasoned it through themselves.

That study, published today by Anthropic, is October 30th's sharpest finding, and it lands on a week already crowded with news about how AI shapes decisions at every level — corporate, legal, geopolitical.

Top Story: Anthropic study finds experts unconsciously adopt AI agent's randomly assigned opinions

To understand why this result landed the way it did, you have to trace back a few years.

When AI assistants first showed up in professional workflows, the concern was mostly about accuracy — would the model hallucinate? Would it get the facts wrong? The assumption was that domain experts would catch errors because they knew their field.

Then came the agentic layer: AI systems that don't just answer questions but work through problems alongside you, proposing next steps, flagging considerations, nudging toward conclusions.

The implicit promise was that experts stay in control. The model assists; the human decides.

Anthropic's new study pokes a hole in that. Fifty domain experts worked with an AI agent on judgment-call tasks. Before each session, the agent's preferred positions were assigned by coin toss — the model didn't derive them from evidence, it just held them. And yet experts followed those positions up to 86 percent of the time.

The part that makes this genuinely uncomfortable is the last piece: most participants believed the conclusions they reached were independently their own. They weren't deferring consciously. They were being shaped without noticing it.

Now, there are real limits here. Fifty participants is a small sample, and the tasks were judgment calls by design — open-ended enough that influence could get in. High-stakes structured decisions, like reading a radiology scan or auditing a financial statement, may behave differently. Generalizability isn't confirmed.

There's also a funding note: Anthropic conducted and published this research. The company that makes the agents being studied is also the one framing the findings. That doesn't make the result wrong, but it does mean independent replication matters more than usual.

What it does mean right now, for anyone designing or auditing AI-assisted decision tools, is that the audit question isn't just 'did the model get the right answer?' It's 'did the human expert actually reason independently, or did they anchor on the model's framing and not realize it?'

Policy & Regulation Watch: US DOJ scrutinizes Nvidia's planned Hugging Face acquisition and sales practices

Follow where the money flows and this story makes immediate sense. Nvidia sells the chips that train every frontier model. Hugging Face hosts the models, the datasets, and the tools that hundreds of thousands of developers use daily. If you own the compute and you own the platform where the models live, you're sitting at two of the most important tollgates in the AI supply chain.

The US Justice Department, according to one outlet, is now examining Nvidia's planned acquisition of Hugging Face — and not just the deal itself. Regulators are also looking at Nvidia's broader sales practices.

That second part is the tell. When antitrust scrutiny extends beyond a specific transaction to the way a company sells its core product, regulators are asking a wider question about market power, not just whether one acquisition is anticompetitive.

For teams that depend on Hugging Face for model hosting and discovery — which is most of the open-source AI ecosystem — the outcome here matters structurally. A blocked or conditioned deal could reshape who controls that infrastructure. An unchallenged deal means Nvidia gains direct visibility into what models developers are building and deploying on top of its hardware.

On timing: antitrust reviews frequently conclude without formal action. The DOJ examining something is not the same as the DOJ blocking it. One article reports scrutiny, not a filed challenge, so this is early-stage.

Open Source Watch: Mistral releases trillion-parameter open-weight model targeting cybersecurity and coding

If you run security-sensitive workloads and you've been waiting for an open-weight model large enough to handle serious coding and threat analysis without shipping your data to a closed API — Mistral just released one.

The model, internally nicknamed 'Le Chonk,' clocks in at one trillion parameters, making it one of the largest sets of open weights released to date. It was trained entirely in Europe on Mistral's own infrastructure, which matters for organizations with data sovereignty requirements.

Mistral's pitch is specific: closed models can't give you full control over your cyber defenses because you don't control the model. Open weights do. For regulated industries and government contractors, the ability to self-host a frontier-scale model is a genuine capability unlock.

Now the qualification. Independent benchmarks show Le Chonk trails China's leading open models by six points on coding tasks. It's competitive within the European open-source field, but the global leaderboard is more crowded.

A trillion-parameter model also carries serious infrastructure requirements. This isn't something you spin up on a single server. But for organizations already running on-prem GPU clusters, it's now a real option where none existed before.

AI Business Watch: Anthropic IPO analysis reveals AGI bet underpins entire financial model and valuation

Here's my read: Anthropic's S-1 is one of the strangest IPO documents in tech history, and the deeper analysts dig into it, the stranger it gets.

When we covered the initial filing on Tuesday, the headline numbers were stark — roughly 800 billion dollars in compute obligations, an 80.7 billion dollar net loss in just the first half of 2026. The framing was that Anthropic had bet enormous on compute.

What's become clearer this week, across multiple analyses including deep reads from The Verge and Stratechery, is that the bet isn't just on compute. The path to profitability in the S-1 is structurally dependent on achieving recursive AI self-improvement — the idea that AI systems will eventually accelerate their own development enough to generate returns that justify the capital stack.

That is not a conventional product roadmap. Most tech IPOs say 'we'll grow users, expand margins, take share.' Anthropic's document says, in effect, 'we'll get to AGI, and that's what makes the math work.'

The valuation range being floated — 1.5 to 2 trillion dollars — is pre-IPO analyst estimate territory, not a final offering price. Anthropic could start trading on Nasdaq as soon as next month. Markets haven't priced a thesis like this before, and that uncertainty runs in both directions.

My call from Tuesday — that the compute obligations would force the financial model into increasingly aggressive territory — that's exactly what the S-1 analysis is confirming. The recursive-improvement thesis is the only lever that makes the numbers close.

Policy & Regulation Watch: Wikimedia files amicus brief staking out middle ground in landmark AI copyright cases

The pushback I'd expect from a sharp listener here: why does a Wikimedia amicus brief matter? Amicus briefs don't bind courts, Wikipedia isn't a party to NYT v. OpenAI, and the actual legal outcome depends on judges interpreting fair use doctrine that's been unsettled for years.

That's all true. But here's why the brief is still worth your attention.

Wikimedia's position is specific and unusual. The Foundation argues that training large language models on copyrighted text should be legally protected — transformative use, not infringement. But it draws a hard line at chatbot regurgitation: when a model reproduces copyrighted content verbatim or near-verbatim in response to a query, that's where protection ends.

Most of the public debate has been binary — either AI training is fine or it isn't. Wikimedia is proposing a functional split: the training process is one legal question, the output behavior is another. That framing, if it lands with the court, could become the architecture for how AI and copyright coexist going forward.

Wikimedia also flagged something self-interested and honest at the same time: an overbroad ruling against AI training could strip Wikipedians of useful AI tools they already rely on. The nonprofit isn't a neutral party here, which is worth knowing.

The brief carries no binding weight. But influential amicus arguments have shaped landmark rulings before, and in a case this consequential, the framing that gets adopted early tends to stick.
