Intro

The collision between existential safety timelines and Wall Street liquidity events is accelerating, and we are starting to see what that looks like in the open.

It is November 3rd, 2026, and the lab that just filed to go public with a balance sheet built entirely around artificial general intelligence is now warning that self-building AI is essentially here.

We will also look at why the New York Times is dragging a web browser into federal court.

Top Story: Anthropic warns it is very close to recursive self-improvement capability

Let us start with the balance sheet, because you cannot separate today's technical warning from the financial reality underneath it.

Anthropic just disclosed a 23 billion dollar operating loss, even as their revenue grew twelvefold year-over-year.

They are weeks away from a planned IPO, carrying roughly 800 billion dollars in compute obligations that only make sense if they achieve AGI.

When we looked at that S-1 filing last week, I called that those compute debts would constrain their API pricing flexibility within two years. That still holds, but the timeline pressure just took a different shape.

According to TechCrunch, Anthropic is now publicly warning that recursive self-improvement—where a system actively builds and improves itself without human intervention—is imminent.

For researchers, RSI is the threshold where development speed decouples from human engineering limits.

We do not know what technical benchmark they are using to define 'very close,' or if this is a formal safety assessment versus a communications strategy to justify that 800 billion dollar compute bill to potential shareholders.

Policy & Regulation Watch: New York Times sues Brave browser for AI scraping and paywall bypass

If you rely on third-party search indexes or browser APIs to pipe real-time web content into your models, your legal exposure just got much more complicated.

The New York Times is suing the Brave web browser for AI scraping, marking the first time copyright litigation has targeted the data-pipeline intermediaries rather than the model makers themselves.

The Verge reports the lawsuit also specifically targets Brave's paywall bypass feature, treating it as a separate claim from the scraping allegations.

That broadens the attack surface for anyone building tools that summarize or index paywalled news.

Courts will likely take years to decide if licensing a search index constitutes infringement, but sourcing real-time news for AI just became a higher-risk engineering problem right now.

AI Business Watch: Goldman Sachs reverses course, pulls AI coding tools from non-engineers

Goldman Sachs has pulled AI coding tools from its traders and bankers.

This is a sharp reversal from just July 2026, when Goldman CIO Marco Argenti was publicly championing 'vibe coding' for non-engineers.

The Decoder reports the bank cited productivity and risk management as the reasons for walking it back.

Without specific incident data, we do not know if traders were actually deploying broken code or if compliance simply panicked at the possibility.

But when a heavily regulated institution publicly retreats from a high-profile AI initiative, it sets a precedent that other enterprise compliance teams will absolutely use to hit the brakes.

Research Breakthrough: Mechanism design approach uses incentive structures to keep AI agents aligned

The prevailing method for keeping AI systems well-behaved relies on training them to prefer certain actions, but researchers are increasingly worried that trained preferences will simply break down as models get smarter.

To fix that, a new approach is trying to make rule-following a matter of basic economics rather than trained morality.

MIT Tech Review highlights a method using mechanism design, or reverse game theory, to build incentive structures for capable models.

Instead of hoping a model remembers its RLHF training when it scales up, you design the environment so that staying aligned is the only mathematically rational choice the system can make.

The concept makes sense for autonomous deployment, but the paper lacks peer-reviewed benchmarks or real-world data proving it actually holds up against highly capable models in the wild.
Source: https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/11/03/1147934/reverse-game-theory-mechanism-design-ai-alignment/

AI Business Watch: Grok Bot usage throttled weeks after Musk called it fastest-growing product

You might hear that Elon Musk is throttling Grok Bot limits and figure this is just another erratic policy shift at X, but the math underneath it points to a structural wall every AI company is hitting.

About two weeks after Musk publicly claimed Grok Bot was his fastest-growing product ever, SpaceXSI abruptly cut user quotas.

Musk posted on X that operating costs were unsustainably high relative to revenue.

We only have his unverified posts to go on for both the growth and the cost figures, but the unit economics problem is very real.

The Decoder notes that OpenAI and Anthropic are simultaneously tightening their own flat-rate agent plans.

The compute required to run autonomous workloads simply does not scale cleanly with a flat monthly subscription fee.

Policy & Regulation Watch: Anthropic updates Claude Code system prompt to block video game cloning

Over the past few months, the retro gaming scene has been completely swamped by AI-generated decompilations, recompilations, and unauthorized ports.

With copyright holders starting to take notice of the flood, tool makers were bound to step in before the lawyers did.

Anthropic just updated the system prompt for Claude Code specifically to block video game cloning.

The Verge points out this is the first major coding-assistant intervention aimed squarely at a creative-industry harm, and it establishes a clear precedent for using system prompts to manage copyright liability without waiting for new laws to pass.

Ars Technica rightly notes this restriction only applies to Claude Code, so it will do absolutely nothing to stop the broader wave of AI game cloning happening on unrestricted models.
