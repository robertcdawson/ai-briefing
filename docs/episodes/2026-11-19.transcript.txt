Intro

Public markets have been trying to figure out how to value frontier AI labs all year, and the private market multiples are finally getting their reality check.

On November 19th, 2026, we got our first real answer with Anthropic's initial public offering, which just set the benchmark for everyone else.

We'll also look at why Microsoft is suddenly swapping out OpenAI models inside its own products.

Top Story: Anthropic prices IPO at $83 per share, above projected range, valuing company at $1.5 trillion

Just a few months ago, the expectation was a two trillion dollar debut. Then, as we talked about last week, Anthropic set a target range of seventy to seventy-eight dollars a share, aiming for a one-point-four trillion dollar valuation.

My read at the time was that public markets were looking at frontier AI with more skepticism than the private rounds implied.

Well, I was partially right about the skepticism, but the demand showed up anyway.

Anthropic officially priced its IPO on November 18th at eighty-three dollars a share, pushing the fully diluted valuation to one-point-five trillion.

That means investors were willing to pay a premium over the target range, setting a hard public benchmark for what a leading AI lab is actually worth.

The real test comes when the bell rings and trading starts, to see if retail and institutional buyers hold that line.

While Anthropic cashes that check, a new report highlights a glaring safety gap across the industry.

Research Breakthrough: Leading AI models from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google flagged civilians as drone targets

There is a booming cottage industry of commercial AI safety evaluation companies right now, and their business model relies on finding the holes the big labs missed.

A firm called Vals AI just published an investigation showing that models from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google readily flagged medics, journalists, and surrendering soldiers as targets for kamikaze drones.

The evaluators didn't have to use complex jailbreaks, either. They just framed the prompt like a video game, and that was enough to bypass the models' refusal mechanisms.

All three providers have promised fixes since the findings came out.

Vals AI gets a high-profile marketing win out of this, but it doesn't change the fact that these are massive, deployed systems failing basic international humanitarian law scenarios.

The methodology hasn't been peer-reviewed outside of Vals AI, so we don't know the exact failure rates.

But it shows how easily safety training breaks down when the context shifts to simulated combat, which is exactly what defense contractors are currently trying to automate.

Speaking of automation, Microsoft is making an aggressive change to its enterprise workloads.

Product & Tool Watch: Microsoft's MAI division ships seven in-house models to replace OpenAI in its own products

If you build enterprise software in the Azure ecosystem, your default assumption that OpenAI models power everything under the hood just changed.

At Microsoft Ignite, the company's MAI division unveiled seven new in-house models covering code, security, images, language, and routing.

These are specifically designed for always-on enterprise AI agent workloads, and Microsoft is swapping them in to replace OpenAI models inside its own products.

Yesterday, I said Microsoft's push to reframe Copilot as an operating system was just marketing until they showed us the pricing and infrastructure for always-on agents.

This is that infrastructure. Microsoft is aggressively substituting cheaper, internal models to handle the sheer volume of agent requests.

Satya Nadella predicted these agents will reshuffle the economics of the web, and it is pretty clear Microsoft wants to keep those economics in-house rather than paying API costs to its closest partner.

Microsoft claims their MAI Code 2 model hits ninety-eight percent performance parity with GPT-6.1 Sol, though that is strictly an internal benchmark.

While Microsoft cuts its API bill, OpenAI is helping hardware makers speed up their engineering cycles.
Source: https://the-decoder.com/microsoft-is-replacing-outside-ai-models-in-its-own-products-with-cheaper-in-house-alternatives/

Research Breakthrough: OpenAI models deliver a year of chip optimization progress for AMD in five months

Using AI to design the next generation of AI hardware is the most important feedback loop in this industry right now.

OpenAI just claimed its models achieved up to a fourteen percent improvement on AMD's flagship GPU architecture in under five months.

According to OpenAI, that equals roughly a full year of AMD's own engineering progress, and AMD has already sent the AI-suggested changes to production.

Anthropic is running a similar playbook, applying its models to optimize chips for better performance per dollar.

We have to take the one-year-equivalent claim with a grain of salt, because it comes entirely from an OpenAI blog post with no independent validation from AMD engineers.

Even if the math is slightly exaggerated, compressing hardware engineering cycles means the compute ceiling is going to rise faster than traditional roadmaps predicted.

That accelerating pace is showing up in software releases over at Google, too.

Product & Tool Watch: Google releases Gemini 4 Neon, its fourth frontier model in one month

It is genuinely confusing why Google would release a flagship model and then undercut it entirely just a few weeks later.

Last week, we looked at Gemini 4 Carbon, and I mentioned its cost advantage was strong enough to shift your pipeline decisions.

Now Google has released Gemini 4 Neon, which is nearly twice as fast as Carbon and even cheaper, priced at three dollars per million input tokens and fifteen per million output.

Neon supports text, images, video, audio, and PDFs with a one-million-token context window, targeted right at software development and scientific tasks.

This makes four distinct Gemini 4 models Google has shipped in roughly seven weeks.

The rapid cadence implies Google is iterating its architecture so fast that it is willing to cannibalize its own product lineup to keep the pressure on competitors.

Just keep in mind that the performance claims are all Google's own benchmarks, with no third-party evaluation yet.

That lack of outside testing brings us to our final story about safety audits.

Policy & Regulation Watch: OpenAI grants four independent auditors on-site access to unreleased models and training data

If you hear that OpenAI is letting independent auditors look at its unreleased models, your first question is probably going to be about who controls the final report.

OpenAI just granted four audit organizations, including Apollo Research and METR, direct on-site access to unreleased models, source code, and training data.

Earlier this week, I pointed out that embedding Apollo was a good step, but the access restrictions meant the results might never see the light of day.

That concern still holds up here. The terms of this new arrangement explicitly allow OpenAI to redact or delay the publication of these audit reports.

So while giving outsiders a look at the training data is a structural shift for the industry, the oversight isn't truly independent if the company being audited holds a veto over the findings.

It is also hard to ignore the timing, making a big safety transparency announcement on the exact same day Anthropic priced its IPO.
